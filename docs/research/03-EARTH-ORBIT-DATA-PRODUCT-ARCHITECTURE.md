# Standardize the orbit boundary, reuse proven engines

This page gives the study on whether earth-plus-orbit data products should reuse ground IT service APIs or build orbit-native frameworks, set out as research findings and a two-sided debate; all sources were accessed on 2026-11-19.

Status: code facts refer to commit `073d4b5` of kamir/OpenTSx (2026-11-19). Statements about commercial products and announced missions come from vendor documentation, agency pages or press releases and are vendor claims unless another source is named.

Mission data architects will mostly rely on common IT architecture patterns, but not in the form "the same service APIs as on the ground", and they should not invent a new component framework. The evidence splits by layer. On payload computers, Linux, containers and Kubernetes-family agents have flown (KubeEdge on Tiansuan, single-node OpenShift and MicroShift on the ISS), and the only data service that answered queries in orbit, rasdaman on OPS-SAT, used the same OGC query language it serves on the ground. Apart from that one case, **no SQL, key-value, graph, stream or object engine has a documented flight**, every hosted-app platform exposes only a filesystem, sensors and a downlink staging call, Flink, Spark, Neo4j and etcd need more memory than a 4 GB flight board has, and qualification rules confine large open-source stacks to low-criticality payload partitions. Two advocates debated the question from opposite ends and finished agreeing on fifteen design decisions: the platform core stays on PUS or cFS, no S3 server and no broker replication cross the link, the unit that crosses is a sealed and signed log segment carrying its own contract, compute travels as signed plans, and Spark, Flink, graph engines and control planes stay on the ground. One question remains open, whether the onboard log inside the payload partition should be a Kafka-protocol service or a runner-owned bounded store, and both sides proposed the same power-cut bake-off to settle it. Of six architecture options examined, the recommended standard is a contract-first boundary with small ground-API engines allowed inside the payload partition, space-native frameworks for the platform, a sync layer only for small shared mutable state, and orbital data-center nodes treated as an emerging service whose developer APIs are not yet public. OpenTSx already follows this design, and its next step is to run the merged bake-off.

| Question | Short answer |
|---|---|
| Common IT patterns or new component frameworks? | **Common patterns plus existing space frameworks; no new framework.** The new engineering is a thin boundary layer (sealed segments, acknowledgment-gated retention, signed plans), not a component model. |
| Same service APIs as the ground (SQL, property graphs, key-value, Flink or Spark, Kubernetes, S3) as the orbit standard? | **No as a cross-tier standard. Partly as local engines.** Kubernetes stays a ground-hosted manager of agents; SQL over SQLite and a local log are acceptable inside a payload app; S3, graphs, Flink and Spark stay on the ground. |
| What does each side say? | Reuse: keep the API, swap the engine, because containers flew and bespoke platforms failed. Space-native: standardize what survives the link, because the only flown storage API is a log and links, radiation and assurance break ground assumptions. |
| How far did they converge? | Fifteen shared decisions (reconciled table below). |
| What remains? | Where acknowledged records live between passes: a Kafka-protocol log service, or a runner-owned segment store. |
| How to decide it? | A merged power-cut bake-off on a 4 GB Zynq UltraScale+ board; thresholds differ on memory, flash wear and recovery time. |
| Alternatives? | Six options from full cloud parity to orbital data centers (table and decision guide below). |
| OpenTSx? | Already a contract-first boundary with one slice of "same API, different engine" (the local Kafka-protocol log); the bake-off is its existing Phase 1 gate. |

Evidence labels: **flown** means operated in orbit or on the ISS with a source, **demonstrated** means ground, lab, emulation or a one-off in-orbit test without reported results, **announced** means plans, contracts or vendor claims. The four debate statements (two openings, two rebuttals) are treated as arguments, not as evidence; their factual claims were checked against the collected sources, and the verification table below lists what did not hold. Background on in-orbit platform services, the absence of S3 in orbit, link budgets and pre-flight test environments is in the companion study [02-WAYANG-FPGA-OPERATORS-FOR-SPACE.md](02-WAYANG-FPGA-OPERATORS-FOR-SPACE.md); only the facts this report needs are repeated here.

## Containers have flown, ground data services have not

The payload tier already runs ground software. KubeEdge and Sedna ran on the Tiansuan satellite Baoyun (Ubuntu Server 20.04 on Arm, 500 km) over a 0.1 to 1 Mbps uplink, and satellite-ground collaborative inference **gained 44% and 52% mAP, returned 90% less data and used about 17% of onboard energy** ([arXiv 2311.06078](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.06078v1)). HPE Spaceborne Computer-2 ran single-node OpenShift on the ISS and finished a DNA analysis in 6 minutes, sending 92 KB in 2 seconds ([ZDNet AU](https://www.zdnet.com.au/article/on-board-the-iss-hpe-demos-the-future-of-computing-in-space/)) instead of 12.2 hours of raw downlink ([Network World](https://www.networkworld.com/article/3656653/hpe-tests-gear-at-the-ultimate-edge-space.html)). MicroShift flew on Axiom's AxDCU-1 with no published results ([Red Hat](https://www.redhat.com/en/about/press-releases/red-hat-teams-axiom-space-launch-optimize-axiom-spaces-data-center-unit-1-orbit)), AWS IoT Greengrass on D-Orbit ION cut imagery by up to 42% ([GeekWire](https://www.geekwire.com/2022/amazon-web-services-cloud-data-analysis-satellite/)), and Unibap SpaceCloud managed 18 flight applications on Docker and gRPC ([NASA NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20230000794)). ESA's OPS-SAT ran **285 experiments from 134 teams with a minimal turnaround of one day**, using GitLab, Jenkins and containerised builds, and its retrospective recommends "a more streamlined and containerised environment" for future missions ([SpaceOps 2025](https://publications.spaceops.org/2025/download_by_id.php?id=0281)). At fleet scale, each 60-satellite Starlink launch in 2020 carried more than 4,000 Linux computers, updated about weekly ([Via Satellite](https://www.satellitetoday.com/technology/2020/06/08/spacex-software-lead-reveals-starlink-details-in-reddit-ama/)).

Data services are a different story. The only data engine documented as answering queries in orbit is the rasdaman array database on OPS-SAT, which stacked imagery into an x/y/t datacube and answered OGC WCPS queries sent from the ground ([Baumann et al., IEEE EDGE 2026](https://peter-baumann.org/Papers/2026-07-13_IEEE-EDGE_SkyFed.pdf)), with no latency, memory or throughput figures published ([EGU22](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU22/EGU22-13002.html)). For SQLite, PostgreSQL, key-value, graph and stream processors the research found **no documented in-orbit experiment**, only forum claims that satellite Linux stacks include SQLite ([SQLite forum](https://sqlite.org/forum/forumpost/1eadc362cc1167c9?raw)). The clearest published blueprint of cloud patterns in orbit, the Azure Orbital Space SDK (K3s 1.29, Helm, Dapr 1.11, RabbitMQ pub/sub), has no public confirmation of flight, and 12 of its 17 repositories are archived ([GitHub API](https://api.github.com/search/repositories?q=org:microsoft+azure-orbital-space-sdk+in:name)). Every hosted-app platform surveyed exposes the same thin set of services (sensor access, position, logging, a "stage this file for downlink" call, scheduling through the operator, an accelerator) and none offers object storage, a database or a broker; nobody offers S3 in orbit ([Azure Space SDK host services](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/microsoft/azure-orbital-space-sdk/main/docs/architecture/runtime-framework/host-services/host-services.md); [Spire](https://developers.sbox.spire.com/space-services-user-guide/index.html); details in [02-WAYANG-FPGA-OPERATORS-FOR-SPACE.md](02-WAYANG-FPGA-OPERATORS-FOR-SPACE.md)).

Footprints decide which engines are even candidates. Physics and assurance add two more filters. Under a proton beam, Linux on COTS SoCs crashed after about 30 seconds on average, with filesystem (56%) and driver (34%) paths dominant, and the authors conclude that "Linux does not degrade gracefully under radiation" ([Memon et al., arXiv 2503.03722v4](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.03722v4)); this is an accelerated test, not an orbital rate. Flight reports repeat the same storage lesson: OPS-SAT saw "partial RAM failures" and "flash memory failure due to wear and radiation" ([SpaceOps 2025](https://publications.spaceops.org/2025/download_by_id.php?id=0281)), HPE's ISS team said "SSDs fail at an alarming rate in space" ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2018/07/26/hpe_iss_supercomputer/)), and Ingenuity's team wrote that "Abrupt power-off can cause file corruption" ([Starch, ELISA 2024](https://directory.elisa.tech/workshops/2024-12-Maryland/F-prime-Michael-Starch-NASA.pdf)). On assurance, ECSS-Q-ST-80C Rev.2 pulls software that shares resources up to the highest criticality among them unless segregation or partitioning prevents it ([ECSS-Q-ST-80C Rev.2](https://ecss.nl/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/ECSS-Q-ST-80C-Rev.2(30April2025).pdf)), NASA requires reused open source to be verified "to the same level required to accept a similar developed software component" ([NPR 7150.2D](https://soma.larc.nasa.gov/STP/DYNAMIC/pdf_files/NPR_7150.2D%20SW%20Eng.pdf)), and an avionics vendor puts DO-330 rigor at **USD 100 to 500 per line against Kubernetes' more than one million lines** ([RTI](https://www.rti.com/blog/containers)). No public ECSS qualification of Linux at any category was found; RTEMS SMP needed a 2018 to 2021 project to reach categories C and D ([RTEMS](https://www.rtems.org/news/2022-02-03-rtems-qualifies-for-the-space-domain/)). The practical consequence, which both advocates end up accepting, is that the debate is about the payload partition at ECSS category D or NASA Class C/D and about the product boundary, not about the platform computer, where NASA's cFS team reports missions that "still run with ~16 MB of RAM and ~8 MB storage" ([Landau, ELISA 2024](https://directory.elisa.tech/workshops/2024-12-Maryland/cFS-Overview-Richard-Landau-NASA.pdf)).

## The reuse advocate: keep the API, change the engine

This section and the next give each advocate's case as it argued it, in its own framing and with its own evidence. Verification follows in a separate section.

> "Mission data architects should build earth-plus-orbit digital products on the service APIs and product standards the ground already uses ... In orbit the API stays and the engine changes: SQLite or DuckDB instead of PostgreSQL, an edge agent instead of a control plane, a single-node log on local flash instead of a broker cluster, sealed and signed files instead of an S3 server." (reuse opening)

**The case.** The payload compute tier is already Linux plus containers, so the open question is which ground APIs to expose on it, not whether ground patterns can run there. Kubernetes-family orchestration has flown with measured payoffs: Tiansuan's 90% data reduction, SBC-2's 92 KB answer instead of 12.2 hours of downlink, Greengrass's 42% cut, SpaceCloud's Docker applications, and Sophia's announcement that its SOOS, which "relies on managed Kubernetes", runs on Kepler satellites ([Tech Times](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/331108/20261119/sophia-space-runs-orbital-data-center-software-kepler-satellites-its-own-hardware-has-not-flown.htm)). The only data service that answered queries in orbit used the ground's query language, from a 1 GB onboard computer to an EarthServer ground federation of "6+ Petabytes", and its authors argue for "a single code base for all deployments" ([Baumann et al. 2026](https://peter-baumann.org/Papers/2026-07-13_IEEE-EDGE_SkyFed.pdf)); no space-native framework offers a query service at all, since the open-source cFS apps are Data Storage, File Manager, Housekeeping, Limit Checker and similar ([nasa/cFS](https://github.com/nasa/cFS)). Separating the API from the engine is ordinary ground engineering: Kine runs the Kubernetes API over SQLite, saving about 200 MB against etcd on a Pi 4B ([K3s profiling](https://docs.k3s.io/reference/resource-profiling)); Versity Gateway serves S3 from a POSIX filesystem ([versitygw](https://github.com/versity/versitygw)); Nisshi serves the Kafka protocol over SQLite at one informal reading of 18.9 MB ([Tansu blog mirror](https://hn.jakechampion.name/reader/https://blog.tansu.io/articles/broker-aws-free-tier)); DuckPGQ brings ISO SQL/PGQ graph queries into DuckDB ([duckpgq.org](https://duckpgq.org/)); and Dapr declares that "All of the components are interchangeable" ([Dapr](https://docs.dapr.io/concepts/components-concept/)), which is how the Space SDK binds its pub/sub API to RabbitMQ.

Operations favour the ground's tooling and failure model: OPS-SAT's one-day turnaround came from CI/CD and containerised builds, ESA's lesson that resilience comes "through reconfigurability and autonomous fault recovery" is the container restart model, and Ingenuity's team credits "Standard user space APIs" for sharing code between deployments and flew 72 times against 5 planned ([Starch](https://directory.elisa.tech/workshops/2024-12-Maryland/F-prime-Michael-Starch-NASA.pdf); [NASASpaceflight](https://nasaspaceflight.com/2024/01/ingenuity-final-flight)). In analog domains the API survived and the implementation changed: Kubernetes flew on a U-2 linked to a ground node ([I-Connect007](https://iconnect007.com/article/125643/lockheed-martin-flies-realtime-mission-enabling-kubernetes-onboard-u2/125646/milaero)), Chick-fil-A runs about 2,800 Kubernetes clusters with pull-based GitOps ([Datadog DASH](https://dashcon.io/2023/breakout-sessions/the-edge-of-observability-how-chick-fil-a-observes-a-fleet-of-2800-restaurant-deployed-k8s-clusters)), and Copernicus serves S3, STAC and OData from a European provider ([CDSE](https://documentation.dataspace.copernicus.eu/APIs/S3.html)). Bespoke platforms failed when they were a choice: Cariad lost **over USD 7.5 billion in 2022 to 2024** before VW turned to Rivian's shipped architecture ([EV Magazine](https://www.evmagazine.eu/cariad-75b-losses-strategic-rivian-partnership-vws-software-division-transforms); [heise](https://www.heise.de/en/news/Cariad-VW-subsidiary-largely-discontinues-its-own-software-development-10712012.html)), and space's own service framework took from a 2010 concept book to OPS-SAT in 2019 for its first ESA onboard use, is called "heavyweight", and OSRA has no flight ([Wikipedia, CCSDS MO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CCSDS_MO_Services); [TALOS](https://talos-5ea2ce.gitlab.io/research/archive/04-ccsds-compliance); [ESSR OSRA](https://essr.esa.int/project/osra-onboard-software-reference-architecture)). A digital product is consumed on the ground, so its contracts and formats should be the ground's: STAC, Zarr, ODCS, OpenLineage, Avro fingerprints and hash-plus-signature traces are files a process with only a filesystem can write ([EOPF](https://cpm.pages.eopf.copernicus.eu/eopf-cpm/main/PSFD/1-introduction.html); [Bitol](https://bitol.io/)). Finally, the space-native camp is converging: cFS plans Rust and DDS and says of Linux "We hope this is changing!" ([Landau](https://directory.elisa.tech/workshops/2024-12-Maryland/cFS-Overview-Richard-Landau-NASA.pdf)), and the PUS Rev.1 draft adds CFDP file transfer with downlink managers ([PUS Rev.1 DIR1](https://ecss.nl/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/ECSS-E-ST-70-41C-Rev.1-DIR1(11December2024).pdf)).

**What it concedes.** In the opening: the platform core stays on PUS, cFS, RTOS and hypervisors; every data row except WCPS is proposed, not flown; the link layer is space-specific, because MirrorMaker 2 assumes a live session, translates offsets lossily and defaults to idempotent producers that KafScale cannot serve; no study isolates productivity gains from cloud APIs in space; upstream tools needed custom work at the disconnected edge; onboard property graphs are weak (Kuzu abandoned, DuckPGQ "WIP"); and S3 in orbit is a naming and landing convention, not a server. In the rebuttal it goes further: S3 is not an orbit API at all, only the key layout travels; property graphs and key-value stores leave the orbit standard, with configuration carried by PUS ST20 parameters or cFE tables; payload health telemetry goes through PUS ST03, ST05 and ST12 into mission control; cross-satellite scheduling needs a space-aware planner, and 1 GB computers should run no orchestrator; and "the cross-tier contract is the signed segment, not any network protocol" (reuse rebuttal).

**Its fact-checks of the other side.** The space-native thesis omits rasdaman; the "~16 MB of RAM" quote describes flight software missions, while the payload boards in the native architecture have 4 GB to 16 GiB and the 2.3 W GomSpace low end is phased out; the native side cites K3s server costs against a design that uses agents (268 MB, or 70 MB for edgecore); "Nisshi lacks transactions" is irrelevant because OpenTSx producers already run with `enable.idempotence=false`, `acks=all` and one request in flight; the cFS claim of 50% savings compares cFS with clone-and-own flight software, not with ground-API stacks; Ingenuity's "Standard user space APIs" are POSIX, a ground API; OPS-SAT ran MO through NMF, but ESA's container recommendation came out of that same mission; YUHENG-OS keeps a container runtime and attacks only the scheduler; and "PUS has had an active issue since 2003" is loose, since issue C (2016) replaced issue A (2003).

**Its revised position.** The cross-tier contract is a sealed, signed log segment with an ODCS contract, Avro fingerprints, a STAC Item and a trace hash, moved by CFDP, BPv7 or a staging call, and the platform boundary speaks PUS or cFS. Inside the payload partition, apps code against two ground APIs implemented by embedded or single-node engines: the Kafka producer and consumer API over an onboard log, and SQL over SQLite or DuckDB. Kubernetes stays a ground-hosted manager of agent-only nodes, and S3, graph engines, Spark, Flink and consensus stores stay on the ground.

## The space-native advocate: standardize what survives the link

> "An earth-plus-orbit digital product should be standardized on what actually crosses the orbit boundary and survives there: data contracts, append-only packet stores and sealed log segments, signed deferred plans, and store-and-forward file transfer ... Ground IT patterns stay the standard on the ground, and Linux, containers and open-source libraries are welcome inside orbit components, but the contract at the boundary has to be set by links, radiation, power and assurance rules." (native opening)

**The case.** The only storage API that has flown is a log, and it already has the right semantics. PUS ST15 packet stores are sized for "the longest non-coverage period plus a mission-dependent margin" and are read from "the last transmitted packet" onward or by time range, which is a consumer offset plus a replay window without a broker; the Rev.1 draft adds a critical log whose "packets not yet downlinked by ground can not be deleted" ([PUS Rev.1 DIR1](https://ecss.nl/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/ECSS-E-ST-70-41C-Rev.1-DIR1(11December2024).pdf)). SOIS standardizes file and packet stores, not queries ([CCSDS 850.0-G-2](https://public.ccsds.org/Pubs/850x0g2.pdf)). Ground control planes and replication protocols assume a link that orbit lacks: YUHENG-OS, simulating 600 to 6,000 satellites, found that "directly transplanting mature terrestrial cloud-native cluster operating system paradigms into space is ineffective", with the Kubernetes resource view lagging **48 seconds and an 82% awareness-induced scheduling failure ratio** at 6,000 satellites against 5 seconds for its own design ([arXiv 2603.27946](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27946)); default Kubernetes lost 70 to 80 seconds per LEO handoff ([HotEdge 2020](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/hotedge20_paper_bhosale.pdf)); KubeSpace binds each satellite to a ground controller and cuts management latency by 59% ([arXiv 2601.21383](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.21383)); MirrorMaker 2 assumes a live session ([Kafka](https://kafka.apache.org/39/operations/geo-replication-cross-cluster-data-mirroring/)); offline JetStream peers stay in their RAFT group ([Synadia](https://www.synadia.com/blog/jetstream-dynamic-edge-clusters-quorum)); and of the ground-orbit data movement methods reviewed, only DTN with CFDP has flown ([SpaceOps 2025, PACE](https://publications.spaceops.org/2025/download_by_id.php?id=0564)).

Radiation and power loss punish exactly the state that service APIs keep: the beam data above, LRO's SDRAM block errors that corrupt many words at once ([Ladbury, NEPP](https://nepp.nasa.gov/files/24662/Ladbury_2013_n224_SSR%20Update.pdf)), LightSail-A's Linux avionics crashing on a 32 MB beacon file and staying silent about ten days ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2015/06/01/lightsail_avionics_wakes_up_after_nervous_silence/)), and K3s's own warning about etcd on eMMC. What survives is small, checksummed state that can be rebuilt from an append-only log. Ground footprints do not fit the computers that fly (16 MB platform computers, 1 GB on OPS-SAT, 2.3 to 40 W payload boards), and the engines that do fit, SQLite and LMDB, are libraries that belong inside a component. Assurance rules make the boundary interface the thing to standardize, because ground stacks can fly only as low-criticality payload software behind a partition, so the interface that matters is what the qualified core speaks across that partition; aviation reached the same split with a certified ARINC 653 core, a narrow ARINC 834 bridge and Skywise analytics over 12,000+ aircraft on the ground ([SAE ARINC 834A-1](https://saemobilus.sae.org/standards/arinc834a-1-arinc-834a-1-internet-protocol-based-aircraft-data-interface-function-adif); [Military and Aerospace Electronics](https://www.militaryaerospace.com/commercial-aerospace/article/55368649/airbus-forms-skywise-subsidiary-to-unify-data-analytics-and-flight-operations-software)). Orbit-native frameworks are open and reused, not bespoke: cFS is Apache 2.0, has flown on **40+ NASA missions**, and its team claims reuse "reduces cost by at least 50% over clone-and-own" ([NASA GSFC](https://etd.gsfc.nasa.gov/capabilities/core-flight-system/); [Landau](https://directory.elisa.tech/workshops/2024-12-Maryland/cFS-Overview-Richard-Landau-NASA.pdf)); F Prime ran Ingenuity on a consumer Snapdragon with Linux; GenerationOne was on 24 satellites by 2021 ([Satellite Evolution](https://www.satelliteevolution.com/post/another-five-spacecraft-launched-with-bright-ascension-s-flight-software-onboard)). Ground-API products for orbit churn faster than missions last: the Space SDK archive, the retirement of Azure Orbital Ground Station ([DCD](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/microsoft-retires-ground-station-services-sells-antenna-to-sli/)), Snowcone discontinued after flying on Ax-1, Snowball Edge closed to new customers ([AWS](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/snowball/latest/developer-guide/snowball-edge-availability-change.html)), Kuzu archived. And disconnected domains spent their money on purpose-built sync (Ditto, Lattice, Foundry, Raft), while ground EO itself is moving from services to static artifacts that a filesystem-only node can produce.

The proposed architecture standardizes contracts and leaves implementations open to any open-source code that passes the assurance gate: packet stores and sealed, signed segments for retention; a typed, static publish-subscribe bus for onboard messaging; PUS housekeeping, events, monitoring and event-action for telemetry; parameter and table services for configuration; signed deferred plans for compute admission; CFDP and BPv7 for transport; ODCS, Avro fingerprints and STAC for the product contract; hash plus signature for lineage; small signed snapshots or CRDT documents for mutable shared state; partitioning hypervisors for isolation; and a ground-hosted Kubernetes control plane with agent-only nodes for fleet management. Developers keep their productivity through a ground twin that runs the flight build, as F Prime code runs on Raspberry Pis "without flat-sat hardware".

**What it concedes.** In the opening: Linux, containers and open-source libraries on payload computers are flown and productive; embedded engines such as SQLite and LMDB fit and are power-loss safe; the Kubernetes API is right as a ground-hosted manager of payload fleets; a Kafka-protocol log can be a sound onboard implementation behind the segment contract; new space service frameworks have a poor adoption record; the trend is convergence; on 8 to 16 GB orbital data-center nodes behind optical relays its case is weaker; and no measured productivity or cost comparison exists for either side. In the rebuttal: assurance rules do not bar ground engines from a partitioned Class C/D payload; radiation faults hit any Linux payload stack, so the defense is state design that any API can adopt; inside a payload app developers may use SQL on SQLite and a local Kafka-protocol log, provided durability is explicit and the cross-tier contract is the sealed segment; an onboard KubeEdge-style agent is acceptable; naming spool files by their ground S3 key is good practice; and vendor churn argues for open standards in general, not for space-native ones.

**Its fact-checks of the other side.** The reuse table marks the K3s app-lifecycle row "Flown", but only the KubeEdge agent has flown on a free-flying satellite; "the Kafka topic API also covers PUS ST15 semantics" is half right, because Kafka retention works by time or size, not by ground acknowledgment, and the reuse design gets that behaviour from the downlink agent; "4 GB to 16 GiB" leaves out OPS-SAT's 1 GB and the earlier verdict that "4 GB is tight for a log plus runner", and quotes only DuckDB's minimum; flown payload tiers are Linux, but containers only in part (NMF ran Java apps under a supervisor, Ingenuity ran F Prime without containers); the U-2 result is a stale demonstration; the reuse side's reading of the radiation paper is right, and it hurts the native radiation argument, but the paper's remedy (read-only root, dm-verity, checksummed writes) is the packet-store pattern; and "both sides propose unflown data services" is only half fair, because the native storage semantics have flown while only its file format is new.

**Its revised position.** The cross-tier standard should be the orbit-native contract: sealed, signed, self-describing segments with acknowledgment-gated retention, signed deferred plans, store-and-forward transfer, and contracts that travel as files. Inside a partitioned payload computer, apps may use ground-style local interfaces as implementations behind that contract. Ground service APIs are still rejected as the standard across the orbit boundary and as always-on onboard services, because "their replication, durability, control-plane and capacity assumptions do not hold for a single, unpowered-at-times, window-scheduled node" (native rebuttal).

## The last disagreement fits on one test bench

The question left is narrow and measurable: **where should acknowledged records live between passes?** The reuse advocate wants apps inside the payload partition to write and read the onboard log through the Kafka client protocol against a single-node log (Apache Kafka KRaft or Nisshi), so that the same operator binary runs unchanged against ground KafScale and in orbit. The native advocate wants a runner-owned, bounded segment store reached through a library, deleting only after ground acknowledgment and following PUS ST15 and ST27 semantics, with the Kafka protocol starting at the ground ingester. The reuse side expects the Kafka API with explicit flush to be as durable and nearly as cheap and to save application code; the native side expects the broker process to add memory, power, flash wear and recovery time while buying nothing, because the sealed segment is the cross-link contract either way.

One fact limits how much rides on the answer. On hosted platforms the question is moot: SpaceCloud's in-orbit demonstration scheduled apps in ten-minute wake-up windows with a 500 KB output cap per app ([Flordal et al.](https://zenodo.org/record/5522872/files/12.02_OBDP2021_Flordal.pdf?download=1)), and no surveyed platform offers an always-on broker, so the result applies to missions that own their payload computer. The other fact both sides accept is that Kafka's default of no application fsync "relies on replicas that a single onboard node does not have" and has to be replaced by explicit flush ([Kafka operations](https://kafka.apache.org/41/operations/hardware-and-os/); companion study).

Both rebuttals propose the same experiment, built on the bake-off that the companion study already defines as OpenTSx's Phase 1 gate. The table merges the two designs; the last column is this report's merged version, with additions marked.

| Parameter | Reuse rebuttal design | Native rebuttal design | Merged design |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hardware | Xiphos Q8-class Zynq UltraScale+, 4x Cortex-A53 at 1.2 GHz, 4 GB LPDDR4 with EDAC, 5 to 6 W | ZU7EG board of the Q8 class, flying since September 2020 (confirmed in the companion study), read-only root, data partition on board flash | Q8-class ZU7EG board with read-only root, dm-verity and an external watchdog; data on the board's eMMC; repeat on a 16 GiB Leopard-class board for headroom figures |
| Workload | Rust runner executing a signed plan of window operators; per-minute statistics of the 1,000-channel, 1 kHz feed, 144 to 288 MB per day | Same | Same |
| Contacts | Five passes per day of eight minutes | Five windows per day of ten minutes, 30 simulated days | Five passes per day, run at both eight and ten minutes, 30 simulated days |
| Stores | (A) KRaft on local segments with explicit flush; (B) Nisshi on SQLite; (C) append-only files with fsync and rename | (A) KRaft with explicit flush; (B) Nisshi on SQLite; (C) runner-owned segment files with fsync and rename | A, B, C, plus (D) KafScale in memory-S3 mode in front of a durable sealed-chunk spool, the companion study's same-family candidate (addition) |
| Faults | 1,000 random power cuts during writes and seals, plus random process kills | 1,000 random power cuts, including during writes and sealing | 1,000 power cuts plus process kills plus forced watchdog reboots, the beam-free fault injection of test rung R6 in the companion study (addition) |
| Primary metric | Acknowledged records lost after deduplicated replay | Acknowledged records lost per 1,000 cuts, target zero | Acknowledged records lost after replay; target zero |
| Recovery | Time from power restore to next acknowledged write | Seconds from power-on to first accepted write | Same metric |
| Resources | Steady-state RSS, mean board power | Average watts, peak RSS | Steady and peak RSS, mean board power |
| Flash wear | Not measured | Flash bytes written per payload byte | Measured |
| Conformance | `opentsx-kafscale-conformance` on replayed topics | Not stated | Required |
| Code | Lines changed to run the same plan against ground KafScale and the onboard store | Not measured | Measured, plus a second consumer app reading another app's output by offset (addition) |
| Decision rule | Zero loss, recovery "well within one pass interval", at most 0.5 W and 512 MB above C: the Kafka API belongs inside the partition | Zero loss, recovery inside a contact window, within 10% of C on watts and flash writes: the Kafka API becomes the onboard log standard | Zero loss is common to both; resource thresholds below must be fixed before the run |

The thresholds differ in four places. On **recovery**, "well within one pass interval" is ambiguous: if it means the gap between passes, that allows hours, while "inside a contact window" allows eight to ten minutes; the stricter reading is the one that protects a pass. On **power**, the reuse side sets an absolute 0.5 W and the native side a relative 10%; on a board drawing 5 W typical, 10% of whole-board power is about 0.5 W, so the two coincide if both measure board power and diverge sharply if either measures process power alone. On **memory**, only the reuse side sets a threshold (512 MB above the native store), although the native side argued that 4 GB is tight. On **flash wear**, only the native side sets a threshold (within 10% of the native store's bytes written per payload byte), and it matters for boards whose eMMC is the persistent medium. The two rules also decide different things: a reuse win puts the Kafka API "inside the partition", while a native loss only means accepting the Kafka API as "the onboard log standard", which its rebuttal already allows as a programming interface. The additions address gaps both designs share. The second consumer app tests the reuse side's actual claim that a topic API gives one onboard app an offset-tracked replay of another's output, which no flown interface offers. Store D keeps OpenTSx's own broker family in the comparison. And neither design measures radiation response, long-term flash endurance or platform quotas, so a pass proves durability and cost under power cuts, not flight readiness.

## Six options, from cloud parity to orbital data centers

The alternatives for building earth-plus-orbit products form a spectrum by how much of the ground service stack runs in orbit and what is standardized across the boundary.

| Option | What it standardizes | Runs in orbit | Runs on the ground | Evidence status | Main costs and risks | Product types it suits |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Full cloud parity in orbit | Ground service APIs with their server engines, manifests and control plane | Kubernetes control plane, SQL and graph servers, key-value, Flink or Spark, S3 server, Kafka cluster | The same stack | **Not flown as a set**; single-node OpenShift and MicroShift flown on the crewed ISS; Space SDK stack ground-only | GB-scale footprints, etcd flash load, control planes break on intermittent links, over a million lines to qualify | Crewed-station experiments; not a product standard |
| 2. Same API, different engine | Ground APIs (Kubernetes API, Kafka protocol, SQL, SQL/PGQ, stream SQL, WCPS, S3 key layout) | Agents and embedded engines: edgecore or K3s agent, SQLite, DuckDB, LMDB, single-node log, eKuiper, rasdaman | API servers and full engines | **KubeEdge agent and WCPS flown**; Greengrass and Docker plus gRPC flown; data engines ground-only | Semantics drift (durability without replicas, S3 without server), Class C/D only, 4 GB is tight | Own payload computers of 4 to 16 GiB; EO analytics and inference sharing code with the ground |
| 3. Contract-first boundary | The crossing artifacts: sealed signed segments, contract, schema fingerprint, STAC Item, lineage, trace, signed plans, acknowledgment-gated retention, store-and-forward transport | Runner, library store, sealer, transfer agent | Every service API: S3, Kafka, SQL, graph, Spark, Flink, Kubernetes | **Transport flown** (DTN on PACE, CFDP); packet-store semantics standardized; ground formats operational; the combined contract is proposed | Two programming models unless combined with option 2; ingester, keys and rotation to build | Bent-pipe missions, hosted platforms, multi-owner and archival products |
| 4. Space-native frameworks | Component model and onboard services: cFS bus, F Prime ports, PUS, SOIS, MO/NMF, hypervisor partitions | Everything onboard inside the framework | Mission control and ground data system; products built after downlink | **Widely flown** (cFS 40+ missions, F Prime, GenerationOne, NMF, SatOS); OSRA not flown | No query, object or stream service; smaller talent pool; MO called heavyweight | Platform core; 16 MB to 1 GB computers; high-assurance and deep-space missions |
| 5. Purpose-built sync or mesh | A sync protocol plus local data model: CRDT documents, bundles with custody, DDS QoS, mesh SDKs, space-aware schedulers | Local store and sync agent per node | Big peer, merge, archive | **DTN flown**; CRDT in 66-satellite emulation; DDS in ground segments and an ISS demo; Ditto in defense demos only | Most engines need live sessions; vendor SDK lock-in; no independent performance data; shared-layer access control | Shared mutable state across many satellites: catalogs, tasking, acknowledgments |
| 6. Orbital data-center nodes | A provider's cloud-like service on GPU nodes reached by relay | Provider compute and storage; customer containers or models | Archive, customers, control | **Hardware flown** (Kepler, Starcloud-1, Three-Body, Axiom nodes); no independently accepted service; APIs unpublished | No public developer APIs, thermal limits, cost, provider exit risk | AI inference over data from many satellites via relay; experiments |

### Option 1: full cloud parity is announced, not flown

Full parity means running the ground stack as-is in orbit: a Kubernetes control plane, database and graph servers, Flink or Spark, an S3 server and a Kafka cluster, deployed from the same manifests. The nearest published design is the Azure Orbital Space SDK reference stack, whose chart defaults allow each service pod up to 2 GB of memory and four CPUs ([values.yaml](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/microsoft/azure-orbital-space-sdk-setup/main/chart/values.yaml)); it ran on the ground and its flight on YAM-6 is unconfirmed. The flown cases are on the crewed ISS with station power and network: single-node OpenShift on SBC-2 and MicroShift on AxDCU-1. Commercial claims of parity, such as Axiom's "Earth-independent cloud storage" and Starcloud-2's "persistent storage and 24/7 access", have no published interface ([Axiom ODC](https://www.axiomspace.com/orbital-data-center); companion study). The costs are the footprint table above, the control-plane results (48 seconds of stale state at 6,000 satellites, 70 to 80 seconds per handoff), etcd's write load on flash, and qualification of more than a million lines. The option suits crewed-station experiments; as a standard for products it has no supporting evidence.

### Option 2: same API, different engine has flown for agents and one query language

This option keeps the ground API and swaps the engine for one sized to the node: the Kubernetes API served from the ground to an onboard agent, SQL over SQLite or bounded DuckDB, key-value over LMDB, the Kafka protocol over a single-node log, stream SQL in eKuiper, property graphs via SQL/PGQ, and S3 reduced to a key layout for spooled files. Its flown evidence is real but narrow: KubeEdge on Tiansuan and WCPS on OPS-SAT, plus Greengrass and Docker-based SpaceCloud. On the ground the pattern is mainstream, and the Copernicus Data Space Ecosystem is its clearest success, serving S3 and STAC from CloudFerro rather than a hyperscaler ([CloudFerro](https://cloudferro.com/cases/copernicus-data-space-ecosystem/)). The risk is semantic drift: a single-node Kafka log has no replicas, so durability needs explicit flush; an onboard "bucket" is a directory drained by a capped staging call; Valkey cannot hold more than memory; Nisshi lacks transactions. Withdrawn edge SKUs (Snowcone, Snowball Edge for new customers, the Space SDK repositories) argue for open engines over vendor products. It suits missions that own a 4 to 16 GiB payload computer at Class C/D and want developers to share code between ground and orbit, especially EO analytics and onboard inference.

### Option 3: a contract-first boundary keeps ground APIs on the ground

Here what crosses the link is standardized, and every service API stays on the ground: an immutable, hashed and signed segment with its ODCS contract, Avro single-object records carrying an 8-byte schema fingerprint ([twmb/avro](https://pkg.go.dev/github.com/twmb/avro@v1.0.1)), a STAC Item, OpenLineage run ids generated offline ([OpenLineage](https://openlineage.io/docs/spec/object-model)), and a trace registered after downlink on the pattern of the operational Copernicus Traceability Service, which uses BLAKE3 hashes and X.509 signatures ([ESA EOF ICD](https://eof.esa.int/docs/api/eof-doc/get-html-sph/45744671)). Compute goes up as signed plans; data comes down by CFDP, BPv7 or a staging call; retention onboard follows packet-store rules. Each component has precedent: DTN is "the first operational infusion into a NASA Class B science mission" on PACE ([SpaceOps 2025](https://publications.spaceops.org/2025/download_by_id.php?id=0564)), packet stores are a PUS standard, STAC and EOPF are operational or in transition on the ground, and no flown example was found of STAC or Zarr generated onboard. The combined contract is therefore proposed. Its costs are a second programming model onboard unless combined with option 2, a ground ingester and key management to build, and the fact that CCSDS security guidance does not name Ed25519 (companion study). It fits every mission class with passes, and it is the only option that works unchanged on hosted platforms, which offer nothing beyond a filesystem and a staging call. It suits archival EO and telemetry products, alert products and any product with several data owners.

### Option 4: space-native frameworks are flown but offer no data services

This option builds products inside the component frameworks that fly: cFS (C, publish-subscribe Software Bus, 40+ NASA missions, Class A test artifacts), F Prime (C++ components and typed ports, Ingenuity), Bright Ascension GenerationOne, ESA's NMF with CCSDS MO on OPS-SAT, Antaris SatOS on JANUS-1, and partitioning hypervisors such as XtratuM, which its vendor says runs on over 1,000 spacecraft ([fentISS](https://www.fentiss.com/fentiss-software-powers-over-1000-spacecraft/)). Its record is the strongest of the six, but none of these frameworks provides a database, object store, query service or stream processor; storage is tables, files and packet stores. Its costs are a smaller talent pool and, for the heavier standards, adoption: the MAL is described as a "least common denominator" ([Wikipedia, MAL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Message_Abstraction_Layer)) and OSRA never flew. The direction of travel is toward ground stacks, with cFS adding DDS and Rust, Space ROS packaging ROS 2 in Docker for a NASA flight demonstration that has no launch date, and Space Grade Linux forming as an ELISA SIG ([ELISA](https://elisa.tech/space-grade-linux-sig/)). It suits the platform core, computers from 16 MB to 1 GB, high-assurance missions and deep space, and it is the right host for payload apps on OPS-SAT-class boards.

### Option 5: purpose-built sync layers matter for small mutable state

Sync and mesh layers standardize a replication protocol and a local data model instead of a service API. The space evidence is thin outside DTN: a CNES, IRIT and ENAC study built a last-writer-wins CRDT key-value service for space situational awareness catalogs and, in an emulated 66-satellite constellation, cut ground uplinks from **66 messages to 1 per update** ([arXiv 2609.18839](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.18839)); DDS is the core of the Artemis 1 command and control system on the ground and was chosen for ISS telerobotics for "tolerance of time delay and loss of signal" ([RTI](https://www.rti.com/hubfs/_Collateral/Customer_Snapshots/rti-customer-snapshot-nasa-het.pdf)); YUHENG-OS and KubeSpace are space-aware orchestrators in simulation and emulation. Ditto, Couchbase Lite, NATS JetStream, Zenoh, PowerSync and Electric have no documented space use, and most assume a live session, so over DTN their sync must shrink to "export a change set as a file, import it later", which Automerge explicitly supports ([Automerge](https://automerge.org/)). Measurement data are append-only facts that never conflict, so CRDTs are only needed for small mutable state: plan assignments, configuration, acknowledgments, shared catalogs. The defense analog adds a warning: shared data layers enlarge the blast radius of weak access control. This option suits constellations with inter-satellite links that share mutable state, layered on top of option 3 rather than replacing it.

### Option 6: orbital data-center nodes have flown hardware and unpublished APIs

Orbital data-center nodes put GPU-class compute and storage in orbit as a provider service reached through relays. Kepler commissioned **40 Nvidia Jetson Orin modules across 10 satellites in March 2026** ([Tech Times](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/331108/20261119/sophia-space-runs-orbital-data-center-software-kepler-satellites-its-own-hardware-has-not-flown.htm)) and has sold IP transport on its optical relay since August 2026 ([Via Satellite](https://www.satellitetoday.com/technology/2026/08/24/kepler-launches-service-on-its-new-optical-data-relay-constellation/)); Sophia's managed-Kubernetes SOOS replicated an alpha build across processors on Kepler satellites, with no performance or fault data. Axiom's ODC nodes launched in January 2026 and two were operating by October, but workload migration was shown only on the ground and the tests do not establish "that a complete commercial computing service has passed independent operational acceptance" ([NewSpace Economy](https://newspaceeconomy.ca/2026/10/07/what-do-axiom-and-keplers-tests-establish-about-orbital-computing/)). Starcloud-1 flew one H100 and ran Gemma ([IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/nvidia-h100-space)), while Starcloud-2 slipped to 2027 and Crusoe's cloud module on a Starcloud satellite remains an announcement ([Crusoe](https://crusoe.ai/resources/newsroom/crusoe-to-become-first-cloud-operator-in-space-through-partnership-with-starcloud)); the Three-Body constellation has 12 satellites with a combined 5 POPS and an 8-billion-parameter model, but names no operating system or developer platform ([eHangzhou](https://www.ehangzhou.gov.cn/2026-07/29/c_298438.htm)). The US GAO called large orbital data centers "unproven", Wood Mackenzie estimated a 1 GW facility at about **USD 170 billion, more than three times a ground facility**, and ITIF expects them to complement rather than replace ground facilities, with near-term value in space-native work such as Earth observation ([Tech Times](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/331108/20261119/sophia-space-runs-orbital-data-center-software-kepler-satellites-its-own-hardware-has-not-flown.htm)). No provider publishes its container runtime, orchestrator or data APIs, and Azure Orbital Ground Station's retirement shows how quickly a provider can exit ([DCD](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/microsoft-retires-ground-station-services-sells-antenna-to-sli/)). The option suits inference over data from many satellites when a relay with availability terms exists, and experiments. It is also the case where the native advocate conceded its argument is weakest, so a documented always-on relay route would reopen the debate for that mission class.

## Analog domains reuse cloud tooling at the edges and build the boundary

Six analog domains faced the same choice under disconnected, intermittent or limited links and safety rules, and their outcomes line up.

| Domain | Ground IT adopted | Purpose-built kept | Boundary pattern | Lesson for orbit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Defense DDIL | Kubernetes, hardened containers, DevSecOps pipelines | Data layer and sync (Ditto, Lattice Mesh, Foundry, Raft) | Local store with conflict resolution over mixed links | Compute APIs travel; the money went to the sync layer; access control in a shared layer is the failure point |
| Automotive | Build pipelines, virtual ECUs, Linux or QNX on central compute, OTA | AUTOSAR Classic and certified RTOS for safety; ASIL D hypervisors | Shared signal model (COVESA VSS) | A bespoke OS built by choice failed; a shared data model is the reusable asset |
| Aviation | Ground analytics (Skywise), EFB and open-world IT | ARINC 653 partitioned core, DO-178C, multicore guidance | Narrow standardized bridge (ARINC 759, 834) and gate offload | Certified core, commodity open world, narrow bridge; analytics on the ground |
| Maritime and offshore | MQTT, containers, Azure or AWS backends | Compress-and-cache edge gateway, fleet manager | Local autonomy with eventual sync over hybrid links | Cheaper bandwidth changed what is sent, not where decisions run |
| Industrial OT | Event broker (MQTT, Sparkplug), VMs, then containers | PLCs and virtual PLCs for control; historians | Shared information model (ISA-95, OPC UA) plus broker | The information model is the hard, valuable part; transport is replaceable |
| Remote science and ground segments | Cloud archives, S3, STAC, compute next to data | Onboard planners, command sequencing, DTN | Batched commanding and store-and-forward | Autonomy pays where uplinks are scarce; cloud economics bite on egress |

Defense converged on "same API, different implementation" for compute and a purpose-built data layer for sync. Kubernetes flew on a U-2 in 2020 linked to a ground node, but no public number exists for update cadence, reliability or certification burden after that demonstration ([I-Connect007](https://iconnect007.com/article/125643/lockheed-martin-flies-realtime-mission-enabling-kubernetes-onboard-u2/125646/milaero)). The Army's NGC2 common data layer, built by Anduril, Palantir, Raft and Lockheed Martin, was declared ready to scale in July 2026 and is "functional but not optimized" ([army.mil](https://www.army.mil/article/294284/army_leaders_next_generation_command_and_control_ready_to_scale)); a September 2025 memo had rated its prototype "very high risk" because any authorized user could reach all applications and data, with one third-party app carrying 25 high-severity vulnerabilities and three others more than 200 each ([BNN Bloomberg](https://www.bnnbloomberg.ca/business/company-news/2025/10/03/anduril-and-palantir-battlefield-communication-system-very-high-risk-us-army-memo-says/)). F-35 ALIS struggled partly because its deployable kit had limited connectivity ([Nextgov](https://nextgov.com/acquisition/2020/07/sustainment-issues-plague-f-35s-logistics-it-backbone/257882)), and NATO's Federated Mission Networking achieves coalition interoperability through standards profiles agreed in roughly two-year spirals, not through one shared platform ([Wikipedia FMN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federated_Mission_Networking)). The analog-domain research found no Flink, Spark, property graph or SQL database at a DDIL edge; edge data stores in practice were vendor sync layers, historians, time-series databases or MQTT brokers.

Automotive split by criticality: deterministic AUTOSAR Classic or certified RTOS for safety functions, POSIX platforms on central compute, hypervisors pre-certified to ASIL D to co-host them ([QNX](https://www.qnx.com/content/qnx/en/solutions/industries/automotive/index.html)), and cloud-native containers (SOAFEE) mostly in blueprints and test benches. The decisive outcome is organizational: VW's in-house OS at Cariad lost over USD 7.5 billion and the Audi Q6 e-tron launched two years late before VW bought into Rivian's architecture ([EV Magazine](https://www.evmagazine.eu/cariad-75b-losses-strategic-rivian-partnership-vws-software-division-transforms); [heise](https://www.heise.de/en/news/Cariad-VW-subsidiary-largely-discontinues-its-own-software-development-10712012.html)). Even with OTA, only 18% of 2025 US software recalls offered OTA remediation ([AutoSoftToday](https://autosofttoday-prod.web.app/news/2025-automotive-software-recalls-record-volume-rework-crisis)), so update capability has to be designed in from the start. Aviation is the cleanest precedent for the converged design: a certified ARINC 653 core, cloud IT in the open world, a narrow standardized bridge, and analytics over 12,000+ aircraft done on the ground from gate offload. Maritime operators kept onboard compute and caching after Starlink cut cost per GB by roughly an order of magnitude (GEO VSAT metered can exceed EUR 10 per GB, against about USD 250 for a 50 GB Starlink tier), and hedged with hybrid or dual-LEO links ([LEOSat Network](https://leosatnetwork.com/guides/starlink-maritime-vs-vsat); [SatNews](https://satnews.com/2026/10/21/speedcast-and-soya-it-complete-hybrid-dual-leo-connectivity-rollout-on-41-wallenius-wilhelmsen-vessels/)). Industrial plants added Sparkplug-on-MQTT, now ISO/IEC 20237:2023, as an integration layer while keeping ISA-95 models, and practitioners say "UNS without ISA-95 just moves the silos one level up" ([Alleantia](https://blog.alleantia.com/isa-95-and-uns-the-semantic-model-for-ot-it-integration)); Audi reached series production with virtual PLCs on VMs while Kubernetes stays planned for apps ([ARC Advisory](https://www.arcweb.com/industry-best-practices/rolling-out-audis-software-defined-factory-moving-shop-floor-compute-data)), and Chick-fil-A's 2,800 edge clusters needed custom tooling because upstream components were not designed for constrained resources or unreliable internet ([Chick-fil-A Tech Blog](https://medium.com/chick-fil-atech/edge-computing-at-chick-fil-a-2621f4b5a969)).

In remote science, Perseverance has at most one uplink per sol, and its onboard planner added **more than 10% science productivity** ([Aerospace America](https://aerospaceamerica.aiaa.org/first-autonomous-mission-planning-system-to-run-on-another-planet-boosted-rovers-productivity-paper-finds/)); mining practice sets downlink priority as safety, then control, then bulk telemetry ([Geomechanics.io](https://www.geomechanics.io/news/article/mining-smarter-with-ai-and-data-edge-network-design-notes-for-engineers)). Space ground segments adopted cloud patterns wholesale, and their failures were economic: NASA's inspector general warned that Earthdata Cloud egress costs fall on the agency and that cost models were not based on operational usage ([NASA OIG IG-20-011](https://oig.nasa.gov/docs/IG-20-011.pdf)), while Copernicus decoupled the API from the vendor. Across domains the winning shape is the same: cloud APIs and tooling for the management plane, build pipelines, non-critical compute and ground analytics; certified or purpose-built frameworks for safety-critical control; and the real engineering spent on a shared data model plus a store-and-forward or sync boundary. Pure "lift the cloud to the edge" succeeded only with modified implementations, and pure "invent a new framework" succeeded only where certification or physics forced it.

## Recommendation: standardize the boundary contract, not the service APIs

The answer for mission data architects is to rely on common IT patterns and existing space frameworks, and to standardize the boundary rather than the service APIs. Concretely, a product team should treat the earth-plus-orbit system as four layers with different rules. The **platform core** stays on PUS or cFS over an RTOS or partitioning hypervisor, untouched by product work. The **payload partition** runs Linux at category D or Class C/D with a read-only root, dm-verity and an external watchdog, a ground-managed agent or no orchestrator, and embedded engines with documented power-loss behaviour; small ground APIs (SQL over SQLite, a local log whose interface the bake-off decides) are acceptable there as programming conveniences, not as standards. The **boundary** is where the team invests: sealed, signed, self-describing segments; acknowledgment-gated retention sized for the longest gap; signed plans naming catalog operators; CFDP, BPv7 or the platform staging call; and a priority order with alerts first. The **ground** uses cloud patterns wholesale, with every service API (S3, Kafka, SQL, graphs, Spark, Flink, Kubernetes) behind a swappable provider, compute next to the data and a budget for egress. A sync layer is added only for small mutable state shared across satellites, and orbital data-center nodes are used only where a provider documents its APIs, relay availability and exit terms. A new orbit-native component framework is not justified: the CCSDS MO concept book is dated 2010 and its first ESA onboard use came on OPS-SAT, launched in 2019, OSRA never flew, and Cariad shows the cost of building a platform by choice.

### Decision guide

| Constraint profile | Choose | Why | Avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bus or platform software, ECSS A or B, about 16 MB RAM | Option 4 on an RTOS or hypervisor | Flight heritage; ECSS-Q-ST-80C criticality rules | Linux, any ground service |
| Hosted payload app (filesystem, staging call, wake windows, output caps) | Option 3 with a runner and a library store | Platforms offer nothing else; 500 KB caps and ten-minute windows | Always-on services; app-initiated IP sessions |
| Own payload computer around 1 GB (OPS-SAT class) | Option 3 plus an option 4 app framework or a static runner; SQLite at most | Footprint | Orchestrators, JVM brokers |
| Own payload computer 4 GB (Q8 class), Class C/D, bent pipe | Option 3 boundary; inside it, option 2 limited to SQLite and the log the bake-off selects; agent-only lifecycle | 4 GB is tight for a log plus runner | K3s server, Flink, unbounded DuckDB |
| Own payload computer 8 to 16 GiB (Leopard, iX10) | Option 3 boundary plus wider option 2: bounded DuckDB, edgecore or K3s agent, stream SQL | Headroom for small engines | Spark, Neo4j, RisingWave, etcd on flash |
| Ground users need on-demand queries of onboard data | Option 2 query language (WCPS precedent) compiled into option 3 signed plans | Flown precedent with deferred, declarative jobs | An ad hoc SQL endpoint in orbit |
| Constellation with inter-satellite links sharing small mutable state | Option 5 (CRDT or last-writer-wins documents as files or bundles) on top of option 3; a space-aware scheduler | Emulation evidence; append-only data never conflicts | Stock Kubernetes scheduling or RAFT groups across satellites |
| Several data owners on one spacecraft | Option 3 with ODRL terms in the contract, checked at plan admission; EDC negotiation on the ground | Sovereignty without onboard negotiation | Onboard contract negotiation |
| GPU node behind a relay with documented IP route and availability terms | Option 6 for inference, keeping the option 3 contract for anything archived | Kepler relay is commercial since August 2026 | Depending on undocumented provider APIs |
| Crewed station with station power and network | Option 1 for experiments | SBC-2 precedent | Treating it as a product standard |
| Ground segment | Ground cloud patterns, same API with a swappable provider, compute in-region | CDSE precedent; NASA OIG egress warning | Single-provider proprietary service APIs |

Four rules hold under every option. No broker replication or S3 PUT crosses the link. No consensus store (etcd, RAFT groups) runs on flight flash or spans satellites. Retention onboard deletes only after ground acknowledgment. Every executable change arrives as a signed artifact, as Starlink runs only SpaceX-signed software ([Via Satellite](https://www.satellitetoday.com/technology/2020/06/08/spacex-software-lead-reveals-starlink-details-in-reddit-ama/)).

## OpenTSx already sits at the recommended point

OpenTSx serves as a reference case because it already implements most of the converged design. Its ground tier uses Kafka-compatible topics named `tsx.<domain>.<kind>.v2` by `TopicLayout` (`opentsx-kafka-v2/src/main/java/org/opentsx/kafka/TopicLayout.java`), produced through `KafScaleClients.producer` with idempotence disabled because KafScale has no `InitProducerId`; Apache Wayang plans on the ground; a Rust runner executes Ed25519-signed plans that name operator ids and never carry code; FPGA kernels are planned behind the runner; and KafScale keeps no S3 in orbit. Its records already use **Avro single-object encoding by default**, with the `C3 01` marker and 8-byte CRC-64-AVRO fingerprint (`opentsx-model/src/main/java/org/opentsx/model/WireFormat.java:5-6`), so every record is self-describing without a reachable registry, which is decision 7 of the converged list. In the six-option spectrum, OpenTSx is option 3 with one slice of option 2, the local Kafka-protocol log, and that slice is the debate's remaining question.

| OpenTSx element | Option | Current state | Next step |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ground topics `tsx.<domain>.<kind>.v2` on KafScale | 3, ground side | In use | Spacecraft-as-site naming in `TopicLayout` (companion study, phase 0); additive changes only on a shared broker |
| Avro single-object records | 3, contract | Default wire format | Onboard runner keeps a local fingerprint-to-schema map |
| Wayang planner | 3, ground | Wayang 1.1.1 with `TsxJavaPlugin` and `RustPlatform` | Add a contact-window term to data-movement costs; Wayang models no platform availability ([Wayang](https://wayang.apache.org/docs/introduction/about/)) |
| Rust runner with Ed25519 plans | 3, compute admission | 57 shared vectors pass on static builds; signed admission covers pass-through only; no key rotation | Signed B2; rotation key; ECDSA option because CCSDS does not name Ed25519 (companion study) |
| Onboard log | 2 inside 3 | Undecided | Merged bake-off above, including KafScale memory-S3 with a durable spool as store D |
| KafScale in orbit | Not used as durable store | Memory-S3 mode is volatile and still needs etcd | Volatile bus only, in front of a durable sealed-chunk spool |
| FPGA kernels | Below the runner | Planned; the design has kernels take parameters from registers | Kernels never touch transport; the runner frames Avro and partitions |
| Link bridge | 3, core | Designed in the companion study | Offset-range sealer with SHA-256 and Ed25519; CFDP, BPv7 or staging adapter; ingester deduplicating by high-water mark and deterministic episode ids |
| Product metadata | 3, contract | Not yet | One ODCS contract per topic; a STAC Item per downlinked batch using the `sat` extension ([stac-extensions/sat](https://www.github.com/stac-extensions/sat)); OpenLineage run ids; trace registration on the Copernicus pattern |
| Mutable control state | 5, small scope | Not yet | Plan assignments, acknowledgments and configuration as signed snapshot files; a CRDT only if several satellites share them |
| Ground S3 | Ground | Durable KafScale profile depends on MinIO, whose community edition is unmaintained | Re-qualify on a maintained S3 store (companion study, phase 2) |

The project's own rules already match the converged design. Producers that cannot rely on broker idempotence make deduplication a property of deterministic keys, which is exactly how segments replay across repeated passes, and the rule that a shared broker is changed only additively fits a ground ingester that creates per-spacecraft topics without touching others. Two gaps are specific to OpenTSx. Wayang's cost model has no notion of a platform that is reachable for eight minutes every few hours, so placement across ground and orbit needs a contact-window term that does not exist in the published literature. And the ground KafScale profile's dependence on an unmaintained object store is a risk on the side of the system that is supposed to be the easy one.

## Conclusion

The question "same service APIs as the ground, or new space frameworks" turns out to be the wrong axis. The unit worth standardizing is the artifact that crosses the link, and the most useful content for it comes from the ground's own direction of travel: Earth observation is moving from services to static, self-describing files (Zarr, STAC, contracts as YAML, traces as hash plus signature), and those are exactly what a node with only a filesystem and a downlink call can produce. Reuse therefore runs in two directions at once, ground IT for formats, tooling and everything on the ground, space heritage for semantics (packet-store retention, custody transfer, signed telecommand-style plans) and for the platform. That is why two advocates who began with opposite theses ended up drawing the same architecture and disagreeing only about one process on one board.

What happens next depends on measurements nobody has published. Whoever first publishes power-cut, flash-wear and watt figures for an onboard log on a 4 GB flight-class board will set the default for the payload partition, and OpenTSx is positioned to be that source because the test is already its Phase 1 gate. The larger open variable is the link itself: if relay providers start documenting always-on IP routes with availability terms for hosted apps, the intermittency assumption behind the contract-first boundary weakens for that mission class, and the case for options 2 and 6 grows. Until then, the boundary contract is the part that will outlive any engine choice.
